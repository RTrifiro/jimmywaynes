Halloween, Seattle, 2006

It is a dark and misty night. Children scurry through the alleys of Georgetown; parents follow, their breath clouding the air.

Jimmy Wayne and Cynthia Ponder sit in their garage at 6929 Ellis Ave S. They pet their heeler Laslo and watch their grandchildren run up and down past the open garage door. As they watch, they drink: bourbon, middle shelf, with a few drops of bitters to even it out. There are some other bottles on the windowsill in the garage. When the kiddie festivities are done, the adults of the neighborhood will wander back by, stopping in for a chat and a shot of something to warm them.

“Grandpa, grandpa!” Ten-year old Kayla comes bounding up. “Look what I found in the garden!”

“Jimmy! I thought you cleaned that out!” Cynthia scoffs.

“I did, dear. Whatcha got there, kid?”

Kayla holds out a dirty, tattered clump of something. The grandparents recoil. “I think it was a wig.”

“At least it ain’t a rat,” Cynthia grumbles.

Kayla lifts a chunk with two fingers, and yellow catches in the fluorescent lights. Fake gold, lost by time, found by the innocence with which only children see.

Halloween, Seattle, 1971

Esther’s breath clouds in the clear, crisp air. She leans back, hands on her hips, and takes a long drag, clearing her lungs from the stink of the corpse below her.

“As in life, so in death,” she mutters.

Two hours earlier, Paul had been sitting in his chair, watching TV. Louis ran out from his bedroom. He was wearing Esther’s white halter dress and a blonde wig, giggling.

Paul sat up, one arm braced on textured beige cloth. “You lettin’ him go out like that?”

Esther swallowed. “Of course not. Louis, go change into your costume.”

When the boy had disappeared back into his room, Paul struggled to his feet. He limped over to Esther, exhaling hot beer at her.

“I told you, you’re going to turn that boy into a fag,” he spat, one meaty finger jabbing at her.

“Don’t say that,” Esther yelled, eyes stinging.

When the back of Paul’s hand made contact with her cheek, Esther clamped her lips shut, hoping Louis wouldn’t hear. He came rustling back out, wearing the costume she had sewn for him. A round red shell, black dots littered across the back, and two perky antenna.

“A ladybug?” Paul hissed. He did wait until the boy was out the door to close a hand around Esther’s throat.

So she went down to the basement. She waited until he came down the stairs after her, swearing and sweating, to where she stood in the shadows with the shovel.

“What are you —” was the last thing Paul Phillips ever said.

Metal clipped wood on its way to Paul’s thick skull. The floor cracked above Esther’s head, and later, Louis would ask why his floor was dippy.

Esther places the blonde wig gently on Paul’s head before she starts shoveling dirt on top of him. “I’m makin’ you go out like this, you fucking piece of shit.”

Halloween, Seattle, 2026

“Tomatoes grew good this year,” they say, nodding.

In January, they became a first-time homeowner. They bought the house from a flipper, saving the land from a grisly, capitalistic fate like the area two plots to the south. The house needed new gutters, a few beams in the basement to prop up the sloping floors, and quite a few coats of paint inside, but now it is a home. Every so often, a small item goes missing and shows up again a few days later. A bracelet. A hair clip. A bottle of maple syrup.

When something goes missing, the queers inside sigh. Jimmy Wayne! They say. Put it back!

He always does.

Halloween, Seattle, 2006

“Oh my,” Jimmy Wayne says, placing the tattered old wig gently on the empty chair next to him. “What a find.”

“I’m gonna go look for more stuff, grandpa.”

“Now, now. Why don’t you settle in here for a second and tell me about school instead.”

Kayla does. Stories, complete with dramatic hand gestures, flow until the rest of the group is ready to go out.

“Come on, kids,” mom says, waving them on.

When Kayla runs off, Cynthia shakes her head. “Throw that thing away. And why do ya suppose you didn’t find it when you did the tomatoes? Think maybe the roots pulled it up?”

“Dunno.”

“Of course Kay would find it. Always getting into what she shouldn’t, that one.”

Jimmy Wayne shakes his head. “They’re a handful, that’s for sure. But sometimes things happen for a reason.”

Later that night, after Cynthia has gone to bed and the kids have gone home, Jimmy Wayne pulls an old, tattered letter out of his pocket.

Jimmy Wayne chuckles.

Louis,

Someday they will find out what I’ve done.

I want you to know that I do love you. So much. Because I love you so much, I need you to understand that love that qualifies things isn’t love. Your dad and you. Your dad and me. I make you my promise that I will love anyone you love; you know this.

If he is found before I get to tell you myself, I don’t want you to be in the dark. I murdered your father. I hit him with a shovel, again and again, until his blood coated the rafters and the metal on flesh made sounds I could never have imagined. I killed him, and I did not feel glad. I did not even feel relief. I felt only one thing, knew only one truth: that some men should not breathe, and that it falls to women to make sure of that.

You will not always live here. It will be someone else, and maybe they will be evil, like your father. I hope not. I hope that whomsoever sees this house and loves it knows. Understands. Believes. For faith is not just for God; it is for justice. There are people out there who know that sometimes you have to be bad to be good. They are the ones I hope find this place, long after we are gone, and I can’t say how, but somehow I just know. They will be like me. Like you. They will be someone who knows what’s really right, even if they’ve always been told it’s wrong.

Jimmy Wayne smiles. He nods, folding up the paper, then stands.

He found the letter in the basement, tucked into a crack in a beam. He keeps it hidden in the old disconnected stove pipe in the garage. Someday, when they are old enough, he will show it to Kay.

See. In this house, you will always be at home.

“Come on, Laslo,” he says. “Let’s go put Paul back.”

Once the task is done, man and dog return to their garage. They settle in, enjoying the quiet as all the children have returned home to organize their loot. The dog lays down with a sigh as the man pours himself a shot of bourbon.

But Jimmy Wayne will not be bereft of human company; oh no. Down the alleys of Georgetown you can hear the echo:

“Come on. Let’s stop by Jimmy Wayne’s for a drink.”
